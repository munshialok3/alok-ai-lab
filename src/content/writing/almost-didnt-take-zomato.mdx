## The offer

In late 2024, I got an offer from Eternal (Zomato). Senior role, growth team, meaningful scope.

On paper, it was obviously the right move. Growing company, fast environment, the kind of role where you could actually see your work in the product.

I almost turned it down.

Not because the role wasn't good. Because leaving AmEx felt like giving something up — stability, global exposure, a brand that opened doors, a team I trusted. The Zomato offer was a better opportunity. Leaving AmEx was a real cost.

This is the part of career decisions nobody talks about honestly: the thing you're moving toward is usually less confusing than the thing you're leaving behind.

## How I actually evaluated it

I have a framework for career decisions that I've used at every transition. It's not elegant. It probably has a proper name in some decision-science literature that I'm unaware of. It works for me.

**Question 1: In five years, which version of this decision will I regret more?**

Not "which option is better" — which one will I regret more if it goes wrong. This is a different question. The regret test surfaces risk asymmetry in a way that straightforward comparison doesn't.

Staying at AmEx: if I stayed and Zomato had gone on to be the kind of role I thought it would be, I'd have wondered for years. That kind of regret compounds.

Going to Zomato: if I went and it didn't work out, I'd have learned something and I'd have moved on. That's recoverable.

The regret asymmetry was clear. Staying had worse downside in the version of the future where the other option was right.

**Question 2: What does this role let me do that I can't do now?**

At AmEx, my scope was global but structured. Large company, long timelines, meaningful but incremental work. I was good at it. I was also slightly bored by the pace.

At Zomato, the answer was: own a P&L from day one, move fast, build things that actually ship. These were things I wanted and didn't have. The delta mattered.

If your answer to this question is "roughly the same things but with a better title," that's not a compelling reason to move.

**Question 3: Who will I become in this role?**

This is the question most people skip. They evaluate the role on what it offers now — comp, title, scope — rather than on what it turns them into over time.

The version of me that spends two years owning growth strategy at Zomato is different from the version that spends two more years at AmEx. Different skills, different instincts, different problems in my head. I wanted the Zomato version more.

This is a fuzzy question and the answers are speculative. But ignoring it means optimising for the present at the expense of who you're becoming.

## What I got wrong in the evaluation

I underestimated the chaos.

Zomato is fast in a way that AmEx was not. Priorities shift. The scope I thought I had on day one looked different by week three, not because the role changed but because the environment demanded adaptation in real time.

I also underestimated how much I'd built in the first six months. When I look back at the WhatsApp Campaign Engine, the Competitive Intel Dashboard, the growth infrastructure I put in place — I wouldn't have built any of that at AmEx. The chaos created the conditions for it.

The thing I underestimated turned out to be the best part.

## The thing about "safe" choices

AmEx would have been the safe choice. Stable, predictable, globally respected. These are not bad things.

But safe choices have a specific kind of cost that doesn't show up on the balance sheet: they remove the conditions under which you find out what you're actually capable of.

You don't know how you operate under real uncertainty until you're in it. You don't know how fast you can move until you're somewhere that demands it. You don't know what you can build until you have a reason to.

The safe choice doesn't give you those conditions. The risky one does — if you pick the right kind of risk.

## How I think about the next one

I don't know what the next transition looks like. I'm not looking right now.

But when it comes, I'll run the same tests. Which regret is worse? What does this let me do that I can't do now? Who do I become?

And I'll remember that the version of me that almost didn't take the Zomato offer would have been poorer for it — not financially, but in the ways that actually compound.

Make the call that creates the conditions for growth. The rest tends to follow.
