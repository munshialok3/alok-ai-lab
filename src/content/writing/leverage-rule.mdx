## The question I ask before doing anything

Before I start any task, I ask myself one question: does this compound?

Not "is this useful?" Not "will this impress someone?" Specifically — will doing this once make the next ten times easier, faster, or unnecessary?

If the answer is no, I'm either automating it, delegating it, or dropping it entirely.

This isn't a productivity hack. It's how I think about leverage.

## What compounding actually means in practice

Most people think compounding is a finance concept. It isn't. It's a property of any system where output becomes input.

When I built the WhatsApp Campaign Engine at Zomato, I spent three weeks on something that most people would have handled manually — swapping templates when Meta paused them, one campaign at a time. The manual version takes 20 minutes per incident. We had incidents weekly. Over a year, that's 17 hours of reactive firefighting.

The automated version took three weeks to build and has required zero manual intervention since. That's 10x leverage. The work I did in week one is still working in week fifty.

Writing is the same. This article takes me two hours. If one person reads it and changes how they think about leverage, and they go on to build something that creates leverage for others — that's compounding. I have no control over whether that happens. But the asymmetry is worth it.

## The trap most smart people fall into

High performers are great at doing hard things. The problem is they apply that ability indiscriminately.

I've watched brilliant people spend three hours manually cleaning a dataset that would have taken thirty minutes to script. When I asked why, the answer was usually "it was faster this way." It was faster *this time*. It was slower over a lifetime.

The bias toward immediate output over systemic output is almost universal. It's also almost always wrong at the margin.

## How I actually apply this

I run everything through three filters:

**Will I do this more than five times?** If yes, it deserves a system. A script, a template, an automated workflow — anything that turns it into a one-time problem.

**Does the output of this become the input of something more valuable?** Writing improves thinking. Thinking improves decisions. Decisions compound into outcomes. If the chain is there, the activity is worth disproportionate time.

**Is this the kind of thing that gets harder to stop doing the longer I don't start?** Learning SQL. Understanding unit economics. Building in public. These have increasing returns — every hour spent makes the next hour more valuable. Avoid them and the gap widens.

## The honest caveat

Not everything can be a leverage play. Some things just need to get done.

The error isn't in doing non-compounding work. The error is in not noticing when you're doing it, and not asking whether there's a version that does compound.

Most of the time there is. You just have to be slightly more patient up front.

## What this looks like from the outside

People who operate with high leverage look lazy to people who don't.

They seem to work less. They seem to have more time. They seem to get more done with less visible effort. This is confusing until you realise that the work they put in six months ago is doing the heavy lifting today.

I'm not always disciplined about this. I've manually done things I should have automated. I've shipped fast when I should have built slow.

But when I get it right — when I spend three weeks building something that runs for fifty — that's when the math starts to feel like cheating.

It isn't cheating. It's just compound interest applied to how you spend your days.
