## The standard playbook

If you work in growth at an Indian consumer startup, the playbook looks roughly like this: run Meta for volume, Google UAC for intent, offer a discount to close, and measure success by install count or first order.

This playbook works. In the sense that it produces the numbers it's supposed to produce. It does not work in the sense that it builds a sustainable business.

I've been inside this machine at Zomato. I've watched the numbers closely. Here's what I actually think.

## The CAC assumption problem

Most Indian growth teams calculate CAC on a blended basis — total acquisition spend divided by total new users. This number looks reasonable. It also hides almost everything important.

The users you acquire through a deep discount campaign have fundamentally different LTV curves than the users you acquire through intent-based search. The users who come in through a 60% off first order are not the same as the users who came in because they were hungry and searched for food delivery.

When you blend these cohorts, the CAC looks manageable because the organic and intent-driven users are subsidising the economics of the discount-driven users. You're not acquiring users efficiently. You're averaging out a bad segment with a good one and calling it a blended number.

The fix is obvious and almost never done: cohort your acquisition channels and calculate CAC-to-LTV by segment. The numbers become significantly less comfortable. They also become significantly more useful.

## The discount trap

India has a discount problem. Not because discounts don't work — they do. Because teams use discounts as a substitute for product-market fit rather than as an accelerant of it.

A discount answers the question "will someone try this if it's cheap enough?" It does not answer "will someone pay for this?" These are different questions.

The teams that use discounts well use them to acquire users who would have converted eventually anyway — they're just accelerating the timeline and paying for it deliberately. The teams that use discounts badly use them to acquire users who would never have converted without them, and then wonder why retention looks terrible.

You can tell which situation you're in by looking at cohort retention thirty days after the discount expires. If it drops significantly, you bought transactions. If it holds, you bought users.

Most teams don't look at this. They should.

## The Meta-first instinct

Meta is the default channel for Indian consumer growth. It's easy to set up, it scales quickly, and it produces volume. It's also increasingly expensive and increasingly unreliable as iOS privacy changes and signal degradation continue to bite.

The teams that have figured this out are diversifying aggressively — Google UAC for intent capture, Apple Search Ads for iOS users who've opted out of tracking, and increasingly, owned channels like WhatsApp and push notifications for retention.

The teams that haven't are doubling down on Meta because it's familiar. This works until it doesn't, and when it stops working, the adjustment is painful because they've built no muscle in alternative channels.

The right time to build channel diversity is when Meta is working. Not when it stops.

## What actually works

I'm not arguing against performance marketing. I'm arguing for doing it with more precision.

The growth teams that are actually winning in India right now are doing a few things differently: they're measuring payback period by cohort, not blended. They're using discounts surgically, not as a default. They're building owned channels aggressively. And they're investing in retention before they pour more money into acquisition — because a leaky bucket doesn't get less leaky by filling it faster.

None of this is new. All of it is underimplemented.

## The honest version of why this happens

The pressure in most Indian startups is on new user numbers. New users are reported to the board. New users make the deck look good. Retention is harder to attribute, harder to explain, and doesn't spike in ways that generate excitement.

So teams optimise for what they're measured on, which is acquisition, which is visible, which is fast. The downstream costs — elevated CAC, poor retention, discount dependency — compound quietly until they're a structural problem.

The fix isn't better tools. It's being honest about what you're measuring and why, and whether those metrics actually track business health.

That conversation is harder to have than it sounds. Most teams never have it.
