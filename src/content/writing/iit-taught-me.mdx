## I studied the wrong thing

I graduated from IIT Kharagpur with a degree in Chemical Engineering. I have not used a single concept from that degree in my professional life.

This used to bother me. It doesn't anymore.

Because somewhere in four years of thermodynamics, process control, and fluid mechanics, I learned something that has been worth more than any specific skill I've picked up since: how to be genuinely comfortable not knowing what I'm doing.

## The environment

IIT selects for people who have spent their entire lives being the smartest person in their school. Then it puts five thousand of them in one place.

The immediate effect is brutal. You go from being effortlessly good at things to being average, sometimes below average, surrounded by people for whom the material that broke you is genuinely easy. This happens to almost everyone. The ones who make it through are the ones who figure out how to keep going anyway.

That recalibration — learning to work hard at things you're not naturally good at, learning to ask for help without embarrassment, learning that being lost is a phase and not a verdict — is the actual curriculum. The engineering is the excuse to put you in the room.

## What I actually learned

**How to work with people who are smarter than you.**
This sounds simple. It isn't. Most people either defer completely to smart people or compete with them defensively. Neither is useful. The skill is learning to extract what they know, contribute what you can, and not let ego get in the way of either. I learned this by necessity. The alternative was failing.

**How to treat uncertainty as a starting point, not a stopping point.**
Every hard problem at IIT started with "I don't know where to begin." The ones who did well weren't the ones who knew — they were the ones who had a reliable process for figuring out. Frame the problem. Find the constraint. Try the simplest thing. See what breaks. Repeat. This is how I approach every new domain I work in, and it works every time.

**How to fail at something and immediately ask what you'd do differently.**
The exam culture at IIT is unforgiving. You will fail tests. The students who struggled hardest were the ones who took failure as evidence of something permanent — that they weren't smart enough, that this wasn't for them. The ones who did well treated it as data. What did I misunderstand? Where did I lose the thread? What would I change? This is just good epistemics, but I only learned it under pressure.

**How to find people worth learning from and actually learn from them.**
Every batch at IIT has people who are extraordinary at specific things. The ones who got the most out of the experience were the ones who actively sought those people out — not to network, but to genuinely understand how they thought. I learned more from late-night conversations in the hostel than from any lecture.

## The thing nobody tells you about elite education

The degree is not the point. The degree is proof that you survived an environment designed to find your limits and push past them.

What you actually get — if you pay attention — is a clearer picture of how you operate under pressure. What you do when you're lost. How you treat people who can help you. Whether you can stay curious when things are hard.

Those things transfer to every job, every industry, every new problem.

Chemical engineering does not.

## What I'd tell someone starting out

Don't optimise for grades. Optimise for the hardest problems you can find, the smartest people you can spend time with, and the most honest possible picture of how you actually think.

The institution gives you the context. What you do with it is entirely up to you.

I got a CGPA of 8.57 and started a company in my final year that scaled to 45 districts across Madhya Pradesh. The number mattered for exactly one thing: getting in the door for my first interview. Everything after that was the other stuff.

Invest in the other stuff.
