## The situation

At American Express, I was handed a brief for a card acquisition campaign in Japan. I had never been to Japan. I didn't speak Japanese. I had no intuition for what Japanese consumers valued, how they made financial decisions, or what messaging would resonate.

I had three weeks before the campaign needed to go live.

This is the situation I find interesting — not the markets I already understand, but the ones I have to build a model of from scratch. The Japan brief taught me more about how I actually think than any project I've done in familiar territory.

Here's the process.

## Step 1: Find the constraint before you find the strategy

Every market has a binding constraint. One thing that, if you get it wrong, makes everything else irrelevant.

In Japan, the binding constraint for financial products is trust. Japanese consumers are deeply sceptical of financial institutions they don't have an existing relationship with. No amount of offer optimisation matters if you haven't cleared the trust bar. Understanding this changed the entire campaign strategy — we led with credibility signals, not offer value, because that was the actual lever.

You find the constraint by asking: what's the thing that, if it were solved, would make everything else easy? Then you work backwards from there.

If you can't answer that question in a new market, you're not ready to set strategy yet.

## Step 2: Talk to someone who is not like you

Data tells you what is happening. People tell you why.

Before I touched a single line of campaign brief for Japan, I spent two days talking to people — colleagues who had worked in the Japanese market, people in the Japan office, anyone who had firsthand experience of how Japanese consumers actually behaved.

The things I learned from those conversations did not show up in any dataset. They were the invisible rules — cultural norms about how financial decisions get made, what trust signals actually look like in that context, what kinds of messaging feel tone-deaf.

No framework survives contact with a new market without this step. The data gives you the what. The people give you the why. You need both.

## Step 3: Form a specific, falsifiable hypothesis before you touch the channel

The temptation in a new market is to run everything and see what works. This produces noise, not signal.

Instead: form one specific hypothesis about what will drive performance. Not "Japanese consumers prefer X" — that's not a hypothesis, it's a guess dressed as an insight. A real hypothesis is: "Japanese consumers in the 35-50 age bracket will respond to social proof from established institutions more than to offer value, resulting in higher application completion rates from credibility-forward creative."

That hypothesis is falsifiable. You can design a test around it. You can learn from whether it's right or wrong.

Running without a hypothesis means you can't learn from the result even if you get one.

## Step 4: Find one metric that captures the thing you actually care about

New markets produce data overload. Click rates, impression share, application starts, application completions, approval rates — all of them move, none of them tell a coherent story unless you've decided in advance what you're optimising for.

In Japan, the metric we cared about was application completion rate — not starts, completions. Because the trust constraint meant a lot of people would start an application and abandon it when asked for information they weren't comfortable providing. Optimising for starts would have looked like success while the actual problem got worse.

Choose one metric. Make sure it measures the outcome you actually care about, not a proxy for it. Optimise for that.

## Step 5: Be wrong faster

The fastest way to understand a new market is to form a hypothesis, test it, and be wrong.

Being wrong in a new market is information. It tells you something about your mental model that you didn't know. It updates your hypothesis. The next test is better because of it.

Most people treat being wrong as failure. In an unfamiliar market, being wrong quickly is the closest thing to a shortcut that exists.

## What this is actually about

This framework works for markets, for new product areas, for new companies, for new roles. It's not specific to Japan or to growth.

It's about having a reliable process for operating in conditions of uncertainty — which is most conditions that are worth operating in.

The instinct when you don't know something is to gather more information before acting. Sometimes that's right. More often, the information you need doesn't exist yet. You have to generate it by doing something, watching what happens, and updating accordingly.

The faster you can run that loop, the faster you learn. That's true in Japan, in Bengaluru, and everywhere in between.
