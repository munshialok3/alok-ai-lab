## The situation

I joined American Express at 22. My first stakeholder meeting had people who had been in financial services longer than I'd been alive.

Nobody briefed me on how to navigate this. There's no onboarding module for "how to be taken seriously when you're the youngest person in every room by a significant margin." You figure it out or you don't.

Here's what I figured out.

## The temptation to perform seniority

The first instinct when you're young in a senior room is to compensate. Use more confident language. Project certainty you don't have. Front-load credentials.

This doesn't work. Senior people have seen this move a thousand times. They can tell the difference between someone who is certain because they know something and someone who is performing certainty because they're nervous. The tell is usually in how you handle the first pushback — if you fold immediately, the confidence was a costume.

The better move is to be precise about what you actually know and honest about what you don't. "I don't know the answer to that, but I can find out and come back to you" lands better than a confident wrong answer, every time.

## Credibility is earned in small increments

At AmEx, I spent the first three months doing exactly what I was asked to do, well and on time. No grand gestures. No trying to impress people with scope. Just consistent delivery on small things.

This sounds obvious. It isn't how most ambitious young people behave. The instinct is to try to do something big early — to announce yourself, to take on something visible. This is usually the wrong call because you haven't earned the benefit of the doubt yet. When something goes wrong with a big initiative (and something always goes wrong), you have no credibility reserve to draw on.

Small and reliable builds credibility reserves. Once you have them, you can take on bigger things with the understanding that if something goes wrong, people know you're the kind of person who figures it out.

## The question is more powerful than the answer

The most useful thing I did in my first year was ask better questions than everyone expected.

Not challenging questions — observational ones. "I noticed that the conversion rate for this segment drops significantly after day 7 — do we know why?" is a question that tells the room you've looked at the data closely and you're thinking about the right things, without requiring you to have the answer.

Good questions signal intelligence more reliably than confident answers do. They also invite collaboration rather than putting you in an adversarial position with people who know more than you.

Senior people, especially good ones, respect curiosity. They've been in rooms full of people performing expertise. Someone who is genuinely trying to understand is refreshing.

## You will be underestimated — use it

Being underestimated is uncomfortable. It's also occasionally useful.

When expectations are low, the gap between what you deliver and what was expected is large. That gap is where reputations are built. I was underestimated in the first six months at AmEx. By the end of year one, I owned a significant piece of the global acquisition programme across four markets. The trajectory was possible partly because nobody was watching closely enough to slow me down early.

This changes once you've built a reputation. People pay more attention. Expectations rise. But early in a career, the underestimation is a window — a period where you can move quickly, try things, and build a track record before anyone decides you're too junior to have one.

Use the window.

## On the age thing specifically

The age gap closes faster than you think.

At 22, a decade of experience feels like an uncrossable distance. At 25, it doesn't feel the same way. Not because you've caught up — you haven't, and pretending otherwise is foolish. But because you've accumulated enough real experience to have real opinions, and real opinions are what people actually want to engage with.

The goal isn't to seem older. It's to do enough real work fast enough that the age stops being the most salient thing about you.

That happens sooner than you expect, if you do the work.

## The thing I wish someone had told me

Being young in a senior room is a temporary condition. Every person who intimidated me in that first AmEx meeting was 22 once. They were uncertain once. They were figuring it out once.

The difference between them and the version of me that walked into that room isn't fundamental. It's accumulated. It's time spent in rooms like that one, making mistakes, learning, updating.

You're not behind. You're just earlier.

Keep going.
