## The rude awakening

When I joined Zomato, I thought I knew growth. I'd spent two years at AmEx running digital acquisition across four countries, delivering $32M in incremental revenue. I'd earned my stripes.

Then I sat in my first Zomato standup and realised I knew one *channel* really well. I didn't know *growth*.

## What hit me in the first90 days

At AmEx, growth meant: find high-LTV prospects, target them through digital channels, optimise the application funnel, measure, repeat. It was elegant. It was structured. And it was one slice of a much bigger picture.

At Zomato, growth means: why did new user order-through-rate drop 2% this week? Is it the homepage? The onboarding? The restaurant ranking? A pricing experiment that someone in another team ran without telling you? A seasonal shift? All of the above?

Suddenly I was in rooms with product managers, designers, data scientists, and engineers — and the "growth" answer wasn't in any single channel. It was in the *system* connecting all of them.

## The transition that broke me (temporarily)

I won't pretend the AmEx-to-Zomato transition was smooth. Those first few months were genuinely hard. Sleepless nights. Feeling like I was drowning in things I didn't understand yet. The pace was relentless — at AmEx you plan in quarters, at Zomato you plan in days.

But that's also where the real learning happened. I stopped thinking like a channel specialist and started thinking like a systems person. Where's the bottleneck? What's the highest-leverage intervention right now? What data would actually change my mind?

## The distinction that matters

**Performance marketing**: "How do we get more installs at a lower CPA?"
**Growth**: "Why do 40% of our new users never order again, and which of the seventeen things we could fix would matter most?"

The first question has a clear owner. The second question requires you to work across product, engineering, CRM, and analytics — often without formal authority. That's the job. And it's way harder than optimising ad creative.

## Why I'm grateful for both experiences

AmEx gave me rigour. Structured experimentation, global stakeholder management, the discipline of working across markets where what works in the UK completely fails in Japan. Real analytical depth.

Zomato gave me speed. Bias for action, comfort with ambiguity, the ability to ship something imperfect today rather than something perfect never.

I wouldn't trade either. But if someone asks "are you a performance marketer?" — no. I'm a growth person. Performance marketing is one of my tools. Not my identity.
