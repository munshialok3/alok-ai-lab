## The wrong way to think about automation

"Let's automate this so we don't need a person doing it." That's how most people pitch automation internally. And it's the wrong frame. It creates fear, resistance, and — worst of all — brittle systems built to replicate tasks that maybe shouldn't exist in the first place.

## How I actually think about it

I automate to free *myself* from loops where my judgment isn't needed. Not to eliminate people — to eliminate the mechanical parts of work that keep smart people busy with dumb tasks.

The competitive intel dashboard I built at Zomato didn't replace an analyst. It replaced the *5 hours per week* that analyst spent pulling data from Sensor Tower, formatting it, and emailing it to stakeholders. Now that analyst spends those 5 hours on actual analysis. The robot does the copying-and-pasting.

## My three rules

**1. Automate the decision, not just the action.**

"Send this report every Monday" is weak automation. "Check these6 conditions, determine the appropriate response, execute it, log why, and alert a human only if something unexpected happens" — that's real automation.

My content pipeline doesn't just schedule LinkedIn posts. It generates episodes using a story bible, validates quality, delivers for my approval on Telegram, handles my response, posts to LinkedIn, pulls engagement metrics, and queues the next draft. I approve with one word. The system does everything else.

**2. Leave a trail.**

Every automated decision should be legible after the fact. When something breaks — and it will — you need to reconstruct what happened without reading source code. Audit logs aren't optional. They're the difference between "the system did something weird" and "here's exactly what happened and why."

**3. Keep humans where taste matters.**

I could remove myself from the approval step entirely. The AI is good enough to publish directly. But I don't — because editorial judgment is the one thing I want to stay human. The system proposes. I decide. That one-word Telegram reply is where quality lives.

## Why I love this work

Here's the real truth: I find this deeply satisfying. There's something addictive about watching a system you built run perfectly at3am while you're asleep. Not because you're lazy — because you designed something *reliable*.

It's the same feeling as driving a car you've tuned yourself. Everything responds exactly how you expect. No surprises. Just smooth execution.

## The builder's advantage

Most people in growth talk about automation theoretically. They make a slide about "operational efficiency" and hand it to engineering. I just build the thing. And because I build it, I can iterate on it immediately when something isn't working — no tickets, no sprints, no waiting.

That bias for action — build it, test it, fix it, move on — is what the mamba mentality means to me. Not grinding for the sake of grinding. Building because the alternative is accepting a problem you could solve.
