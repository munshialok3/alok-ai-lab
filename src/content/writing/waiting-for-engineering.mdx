## The queue

Every growth team I've seen operates with some version of the same dynamic: the growth person has an idea, writes a brief, submits a ticket, and waits.

The wait is usually measured in weeks. Sometimes months. During that wait, the market doesn't pause. The competitor doesn't pause. The opportunity window, which was open when the idea emerged, may or may not still be open when the ticket finally ships.

This is the hidden cost. Not the delay itself — the compounding of delays over time.

## What actually decays while you wait

**Your hypothesis.** The idea you had was based on a specific reading of the data at a specific moment. Markets move. User behaviour shifts. By the time your test ships, the conditions that generated the hypothesis may no longer exist. You're testing an answer to a question nobody is asking anymore.

**Your momentum.** Experimentation has a flywheel. Running tests produces learnings. Learnings generate new hypotheses. New hypotheses produce better tests. When you slow the cycle — even by two weeks — you're not just losing those two weeks. You're losing the compounding effect of everything that would have followed.

**Your ownership.** When someone else builds the thing you thought of, you become a consumer of it rather than an author of it. The feedback loop between idea and implementation disappears. You stop developing intuition for what's technically possible because you've outsourced that judgment entirely.

## The argument for building it yourself

I'm not suggesting every growth person needs to become an engineer. That's not realistic and it's not the point.

The point is that there's a category of problem — infrastructure problems, automation problems, tooling problems — where the growth person is the most motivated person in the room to solve it, and often has enough technical surface area to do it without a dedicated engineering ticket.

I built the WhatsApp Campaign Engine at Zomato because I was the one who felt the pain every time a template got paused at 2am. No engineer was losing sleep over it. I was. That asymmetry of motivation is exactly when you should build it yourself.

I built a competitive intelligence dashboard because I was the one sitting through the weekly manual reporting process, watching five hours of productive capacity evaporate. I was motivated to make it stop in a way that no one else on the team was.

Motivation plus sufficient technical capability is a remarkably powerful combination. Most growth people undersell the capability side because they've never tested it.

## The capability question is usually wrong

"I don't know how to build that" is usually not the real constraint. The real constraint is "I've never tried to figure out how to build that."

These are different things. The first is a fixed state. The second is a decision you're making about how to spend the next few days.

I learned Cloudflare Workers by reading documentation and breaking things. I learned the Meta Graph API by reading documentation and breaking things. Neither required a formal background in those technologies. They required curiosity and a specific problem I was motivated to solve.

The moment you have a concrete problem you care about, learning becomes significantly easier. You're not learning in the abstract — you're learning in order to make a specific thing stop being broken.

## What this looks like in practice

You don't have to build everything. Most things should still go through engineering — the ones that require scale, reliability, or architectural decisions above your technical level.

But before you write the ticket, ask: is there a version of this I could build myself in a week that would give me 80% of the value? Not the production-grade version. The version that solves the problem now, that proves the concept, that removes the dependency.

Sometimes the answer is no. But more often than most growth people expect, the answer is yes.

And the version you build yourself ships this week, not in six.

## The compounding advantage

Growth people who can build accumulate something over time that their non-building peers don't: a direct relationship between ideas and outcomes.

They know what's technically hard and what isn't. They know where the real constraints are. They develop intuition for what's worth building and what's scope creep. They stop being surprised by implementation timelines because they've lived them.

This makes them dramatically better at the strategic side of growth — not because building is the point, but because understanding constraints is the point, and building is the fastest way to understand them.

The hidden cost of waiting for engineering isn't just the delay. It's everything you don't learn while you're waiting.
